# Sage on the desk: what is done

What this project has, how each piece came and how it was checked. The roadmap holds only what is left (`docs/ROADMAP.md`).

## The start (2026-11-16)

- The engine is Joe Bentham's desk-buddy (MIT) with Chris Simpkins's fork on top (MIT): the firmware for the Waveshare ESP32-S3-LCD-1.3 prism (240 by 240, RGB565, a QMI8658 motion sensor for taps), the Mac bridge that turns Claude Code's hooks into moods, frames stored as Canvas 2D drawing instructions that the board renders itself (Chris's: a frame of about 1 KB instead of 8), clips over Wi-Fi without flashing, and reactions to what the terminal runs.
- All of Lump's art left with the first commit (his drawings, clips, sounds, the menu bar app with his frames and the clip skill written for him), and Sage's license came in: all rights reserved for Sage, MIT for the rest.

## The emulator (2026-11-16)

The test bed while there is no board (`emulator/`, its README says how to run it).

- `emulator/board.mjs` is the firmware's `main.cpp` in JavaScript, each method naming the function it mirrors: the file's parser, the state graph (`nextHop`, `settle`, `setTarget`), events, taps and their gags, `autoBehaviour`, `runOffline` with its unplugged mark, the corner marks, the backlight, the line protocol, the demo and the clip store (32, the one played longest ago evicted); its loop runs on wall-clock time, so slow frames are dropped as on the board. `emulator/vec.mjs` is `vec.h` line for line, into RGB565, so the page shows the board's own pixels.
- The page (`emulator/index.html`): the prism, a click as a tap, the board's readouts (frame rate, draw time, backlight, colour, counts, clips), its serial log and a command box.
- The bridge's third link (`--emulator`, or `"emulator": true` in its config): it serves the page, sends each line of the protocol as a Server-Sent Event, takes the board's replies by POST and delivers clips as `POST /clip` would, with nothing new to install; serial and TCP behave as before.
- A test card (`emulator/testcard/`): an abstract shape a mood, with transitions and events, baked by the bake tool with its new `--page` option (787 frames, all instructions, 152 KB), so the emulator and its tests have real frames and no character's art.
- Checked: 36 JavaScript tests (parsing, routes, the timeouts, offline, taps, clip eviction, colour and backlight) and 93 Python ones (76 that were there, which now run without pyserial, and 17 new); the test card against `vec_check.cpp`, the firmware's renderer compiled natively: 783 of 787 frames identical and the other 4 within 0.005% (the tolerance is 2%), and every frame of a clip identical; and headless Chromium against the real bridge, hooks sent as the installed ones send them: a hook changed the page's mood in 25 ms and reached its loop in 352 ms, the page ran at 23.9 frames a second, a click reached the bridge as a tap in 20 ms, a clip arrived and played in 354 ms, and night dimmed the backlight.
- Not checked without the board: its real frame rate and draw times, the corner marks' exact pixels, the prism's visible area. The emulator has no Wi-Fi clock (night offline comes from the bridge's heartbeat) and models a click as one clean jolt.

## Sage's first drawing (2026-11-16)

- `design/sage.html` plays Sage's moods, transitions and acts as the board plays them, in a prism, and lays them out to review with `?sheet`; it exposes the interface the bake reads. `design/sage-draw.js` draws Sage from the 3D model's measurements with the website's own face, through a layer that turns `arcTo` into the line and arc it traces and the glow into flat layers, so every frame is the board's instructions. `design/sage-acts.js` holds the board's ten moods as Sage lives them, the transitions in and out of idle, and eight acts.
- Measured in the page: 36 sequences, 395 drawings, 72 seconds of animation, 463 unique frames, every canvas call one the board can draw, about 1.8 KB a frame and 825 KB in all.
- Three looks were set side by side on the prism (a sheet: illustrated, the room's 3D as JPEG, glyphs); the author chose the illustrated one for everyday and the glyphs for its special moments, and no 3D for now.

## Sage in a small 3D world (2026-11-16)

The author asked for a Sage that turns and is seen from the front, the side and behind, for a camera that plays (from above, from below), and for motion with a cartoon's licence.

- `design/sage-3d.js` is a small 3D engine drawn with the board's instructions: rotation matrices, parts hung on one another in frames (the feet and legs on the floor, the body leaning about its hips, the head turning, rolling and bowing on its neck) and a perspective camera that orbits, tilts and comes in close. A box's outline is the convex hull of its rounded core, grown by its radius; what is on a face is drawn in that face's own millimetres through the exact map of its plane; shapes are drawn far to near.
- A first version placed flat shapes in 3D by approximation. Turned and seen from the side it was wrong (the author: the camera was bad), and it was rebuilt as the engine above.
- The shading had two looks. The first stepped each part's whole silhouette in smaller, lighter copies toward the light, with a dark quad over each face turned from it. From the front it read, but as the camera moved it showed contour rings, and grey rectangles on the back and the top; the author called the free camera's GIFs horrible. Now each part is lit as a soft cube: every face that shows in one tone, from a key light that mostly rides with the camera (three quarters) and partly stays in the world (a quarter), with a little fill from the camera; a band in the tone between two faces where they meet, as wide as the part's radius looks there; a soft light where the key falls on a face and a specular spot near its lit edge; and what is on a face clipped to the silhouette, so the visor no longer pokes out past the head seen side-on.
- Motion with a cartoon's licence (`cartoon()` in `design/sage-acts.js`): springs run tick by tick over each act, so the antenna trails the head and whips past when it stops, the body stretches as it rises and squashes as it lands, the head rolls behind a sideways start, and the eyes blink on a quick turn. A loop runs twice and keeps the second time round, so it closes on itself.
- On twos: a drawing is held for two ticks, as cartoons are animated, except where Sage itself moves fast (a hop, a double take, a quick turn), which goes on ones. The camera always moves on twos, and what moves with time (the visor, the sleep wave) moves on even ticks, so a held drawing is the same frame twice. With the same acts it took the unique instruction frames from 2,159 to 1,514 (measured in the page).
- The camera's acts are about Sage minding the camera. Going round it, its head keeps you in sight (up to 0.9 rad) and its body catches up in three little hops; on a crane it looks down at the camera when it is low, lifts its head to follow it up, and hops for it at the top; from straight above it looks up into the camera while a glider crawls by on the desk; over its shoulder it reads, feels the camera behind it, and looks back. A block, a beehive and a blinker sit on a faint board round it, so the world turns as the camera does. The orbit had been rounded to steps of 0.02 rad while it moved 0.044 a tick, so it went forward in alternating steps of 0.04 and 0.06: a judder in the GIFs, gone now.

## Its desk is a board of the Game of Life (2026-11-16)

- What came into Sage's world for the author's work started as stock metaphors: a bug for failing tests, a rocket for a deploy, crates for a build. Drawn again in Sage's own materials (amber voxels, the website's prompt on a column of light, cream modules) they still read as a bug, a rocket and boxes, and the author said so. Now its desk is a board of the Game of Life, the website's own world, and the work shows up as Life on it.
- A test run lights a grid of cells one by one, and a failing test comes up amber and will not hold still. Sage glances back at you as if nothing happened, does a double take, leans in to look, and turns to tell you.
- A deploy is a glider, the website's mark, put together cell by cell while the visor counts them. Sage gives it a nudge and it sets off by Life's own rule (`SageThings.life`, B3/S23) off the board and away, the camera following it a little.
- A build is a seed of eight cells computed generation by generation until, after 17 generations, it settles into two loaves. Sage flinches when the pattern boils over and is pleased when it settles. The seed came from a search of 400,000 random 5 by 5 seeds for one that settles into still lifes of at least eight cells, within a 10 by 8 box, in 16 to 40 generations.
- The cells are small blocks lit as Sage is, on a faint grid, the camera risen over the desk so the board reads. The room's phone stays: it is the room's own.

## The rain's repertoire (2026-11-16)

- Thirteen glyph acts, each its own idea: made of the rain going about its idle; just its eyes in the dark, then the rest of it round them; falling apart into the rain and back; blown away to the right and back in from the left; turning round; ASCII art, a line coming down it that turns the rain into a density ramp in a monospaced font; scrambled from the bottom up and decoded row by row from the top; a scan passing down and up with its eyes following it; tests failing, torn like a bad signal and amber; so happy it flies apart and gathers again; dozing in a slow, thin rain with z's rising beside its head; rained back in from the top; thinking, glyphs streaming down into its head.
- Its hand-over between the illustrated Sage and its glyphs is a tear like a bad signal, all in glyph frames. It replaced laying one over the other with `drawImage`, whose 66 pixel frames took 2.3 MB.
- Each glyph drawing carries its own time, so a drawing held two ticks is one frame, and the acts run on twos.

## All of it baked again (2026-11-16)

- `firmware/data/sage.bin`: 10,725,857 bytes, 86.1% of the partition, 1,725,983 left. 59 sequences (10 moods, 18 transitions, 31 acts), 2,509 unique frames: 1,496 as instructions (8,770,496 bytes), 1,013 as glyphs (1,929,906 bytes, from 480 to 3,448 a frame, median 1,915, up to 492 glyphs), none as pixels; an atlas of 90 glyphs (the rain's, and the ASCII ramp in a second font) in 3,454 bytes. Before this it was 13,647,844 bytes, 1.2 MB over the partition.

Left out below a third of its light, where it cannot be seen, every instruction frame matches. Why `vec.h` draws that faint wash differently was not looked into.

The 81 dimmest glyph frames (under 2,500 lit pixels) are seven runs where Sage is meant to be gone or nearly (its eyes alone, fallen apart, blown away), and none on its own.
- The gallery's 28 GIFs drawn again through the bake (`tools/export_frames.py --clip NAME --gif`).

## Glyph frames (2026-11-16)

A third kind of frame, for the acts where Sage is made of the rain's glyphs (`design/sage-glyphs.js`). Text is not one of the board's instructions, so those frames were stored as pixels, 19 to 39 KB each: the four glyph acts alone (404 frames) would take 12,140,895 bytes, and Sage's art with them about 20.0 MB, against an art partition of 12,451,840.

- The format, version 6 (`tools/export_frames.py` documents it): a glyph frame is a background colour and, for each glyph, the whole pixel the browser drew its anchor at, an index into the file's atlas and its colour in RGB565 with its alpha folded in against black, 7 bytes a glyph. The atlas holds every text the art drew, rendered alone by the page in the same font, as 4-bit coverage with its offset from the anchor. The top two bits of a frame's offset now say its kind (pixels, instructions, glyphs); the board and the emulator still read versions 3 to 5. A clip carries its own atlas.
- The bake: a frame that is only a whole-screen opaque fill and text (`fillText` with `fillStyle` and `globalAlpha`) becomes a glyph frame if `firmware/src/glyphs.h`, built for the Mac as `tools/glyph_check.cpp`, reproduces the browser's picture within the instruction frames' tolerance (a mean of 2% of full scale, 300 pixels far off); anything else is kept as before. Chrome places text on whole pixels vertically and on quarter pixels across, so the atlas is rendered at a whole pixel and, per font, the bake measures the fraction past which a falling glyph moves one pixel down (0.078 for Sage's 9 px bold Hiragino Sans, the font's own middle offset); Sage's glyphs sit on whole pixels across.
- 4 bits against 1: the same 404 frames redrawn with the coverage cut to 1 bit, 82 stay within the tolerance (a median of 1,070 pixels far off); with 4 bits all 404 do.
- `firmware/src/glyphs.h` draws a frame into the sprite: the background, then each glyph's coverage blended in its colour, clipped to the screen, and a glyph naming a bitmap the atlas does not have skipped. The parser checks every atlas record against the atlas and every frame's kind and length against the partition, as it does the rest. `emulator/glyphs.mjs` is the same, line for line.
- Sage baked whole (`firmware/data/sage.bin`): 8,644,584 bytes, 69% of the partition; 1,630 unique frames, 1,160 as instructions, 404 as glyphs and 66 as pixels (the hand-over, which lays the illustrated Sage over its glyphs with `drawImage`: 2,193,312 bytes, a quarter of the file). Glyph frames run from 1,299 to 2,468 bytes (median 1,957), 767,906 in all, with an atlas of 78 glyphs in 3,162 bytes; the largest has 352 glyphs. The worst glyph frame is 0.52% from Chrome's picture with 52 pixels far off; the worst instruction frame 0.708% and 102.
- Checked: `node --test` in `emulator/` (44 tests, 8 of them new: version 6 parsed, every truncation and every kind of damage refused cleanly, known pixels, clipping at the edges, drawing order, a clip's own atlas, and the emulator against `glyphs.h` on 300 glyphs) and the bridge's 93 Python tests; the test card baked again, its 787 frames byte for byte the same as before; `emulator/checks/glyph_fidelity.mjs` on Sage's art with the bake's references (`--refs`): the emulator identical to `glyphs.h` on all 404 glyph frames, and against Chrome a median of 0.457% and at worst 0.52% and 52 pixels; `vec_fidelity.mjs` on Sage's 1,160 instruction frames, 1,000 identical and the rest within 0.004%.
- Played end to end: the bridge in emulator mode with `--art firmware/data/sage.bin`, the page in headless Chromium, `e glyph_form`, `e glyph_mode` and `e glyph_dissolve` sent through the bridge's `/cmd`: each played its 4 or 5 seconds with the page counting 24.0 frames a second, glyph frames throughout and pixels only for the hand-over.
- The firmware compiled for the first time on this machine, with PlatformIO 6.2.0 in `tools/.venv` (git-ignored): espressif32 8.0.0, Arduino core 3.3.12, LovyanGFX 1.3.0. Before the change it built with 106,972 bytes of RAM (32.6%) and 1,136,767 of flash (54.2%); after, 107,500 (32.8%) and 1,137,819 (54.3%), with the same six warnings and none from the new code. Nothing was flashed.
- Not checked without the board: how long a glyph frame takes to draw there, and so whether the largest (352 glyphs, 22,696 bitmap pixels visited, 9,944 of them blended, after a background fill of 57,600) holds 24 frames a second.

## Nothing lost, and what Sage projects (2026-11-16)

- Redoing the three acts for the author's work as Life on the desk replaced the originals, and the hearts had gone for a glider before that; the author asked for them back ("no debemos perder cosas") and said Life is only one part of the animations. Now `work_tests_fail` (a glitch scuttles in and Sage stomps it), `work_deploy` (the website's prompt takes off) and `work_build` (its own modules land on the pile) are back as they were, drawn with the new lighting; the Life ones stay beside them as `life_tests_fail`, `life_deploy` and `life_build`. `done_hearts` is back with the room's own heart: its eyes red hearts, beating, as the room's Sage shows when it loves what it sees. `glyph_form` (out of the rain, for waking) and `glyph_dissolve` (into it, for when the bridge is gone) are acts of their own again, tearing only where they meet the illustrated Sage. The rule went into CLAUDE.md: an act redone keeps the old one as a variant.
- Holograms (`design/sage-holo.js`). The first version threw a panel from the edge of the visor: a dark green card with a border, scan lines laid over it and a filled funnel of light to it. The author: horrible, not achieved. It was rebuilt as light only, on a sheet of three proposals the author approved:
- Every hologram comes from a lens on the top edge of Sage's head, near the front right corner (a graphite ring, dark glass, a green glint, lit while it projects), as the author asked: the projector is a point on its head. It is the one detail of Sage's body that is not in the 3D model.
- A hologram is made only of light: no panel behind it; its strokes glow in three layers (a wide faint halo, a narrower one, the bright core); it exists only where its scan lines are; a band of light runs down it brightening what it passes over; now and then it dims, and a slice of it jumps sideways (more when something fails). It opens as the room's screens do, a point, a line, then the picture from its middle, and closes the same way. A soft cone of light in faint layers and a few rays runs from the lens to it.
- Three places: standing on the desk, on a shape of light the lens throws there (the shape suits what it shows: a rectangle for the tests and the battery, a square for a Life board, a line for the clock, a ring for a build's progress and for itself; the author: no shape is a rule); floating in the air beside it; or taking the whole screen, where Sage bows its lens to you, the lens flares in rings of light while the room goes dark, the picture fills the screen until it is done, closes to a line and a point, and Sage is back.
- Eight acts: the tests as they run and a wireframe of itself, on the desk; a build's progress, the time late at night, its battery below a quarter and a glider going round a board, in the air; the tests and itself taking the whole screen. The numbers they show are fixed in the drawings.
- The Life boards on the desk are holograms too, thrown there from the lens: their grid of glowing light, a band sweeping across them, a flicker and the cone from the lens; the board round Sage in the camera's acts glows the same way without a cone.


## Sage's mind (2026-11-16)

The bridge was written for Lump: fixed rules from a hook to a mood, a list of gags and clips sent over Wi-Fi, none of them Sage's. The author decided that Sage's mind runs as a small Node process beside the Python bridge, because the website's mind is TypeScript and its patience and mood come as the bundle the art already uses: one Sage, never copied. The bridge keeps the board's link, the hooks, the commands it reads, the calendar, Slack, the usage and the sounds, and the board's moods as it set them before; the mind chooses the acts.

- `mind/` is plain Node with no dependencies, in five pieces: the table of reactions as data (`table.mjs`, where a new reaction is one entry), the engine that chooses (`mind.mjs`), the website's code loaded as it is in a `vm` context (`site.mjs`), the names of the art's acts read with the emulator's own parser (`art.mjs`, so the format's version 7 is read as soon as the emulator reads it), and the protocol adapter (`sage-mind.mjs`). Asked how to keep the bridge, the render and the mind easy to maintain, the answer was to keep it modular by its contracts, and no further: the contract between the bridge and the mind is `mind/README.md`, protocol 1 in the first message, and the contract with the art is only the names of its acts. A first version routed signals to reactions in a `switch` in the engine; the routing moved into the table so that adding a reaction changes no code.
- The protocol: JSON lines on the mind's stdin and stdout, one answer a line, in order, each with its id. The bridge sends the kind of thing that happened and only the context a reaction needs (`ok`, `busy`, `around`, `late`, `battery`, the board's mood), never a command, a prompt or a path of the author's work; the mind answers with an act the art holds, or none, and why. It logs nothing.
- The table is Sage's character as the author wrote it: tests failing, a celebration (tests passing and a quiet Stop share its ten minutes), builds that worked and failed, a push, a deploy, a merge conflict, a compaction, a hello after a long quiet, waiting for the author, idling about once a minute (mostly fidgets, now and then the camera, a hologram or the rain, only fidgets while the author is away), late at night (the clock once a night, dozing now and then), its battery falling below a quarter, and a tap. Each has a priority (work, then the author, then late at night, then idling), a cooldown and weights; no act is chosen again within the last six while at least two others are left to weigh, otherwise as the website picks a pastime (each the less likely the sooner it was chosen, never the one just chosen). Asleep or at a meeting's alarm only taps play, and while something needs the author only looking at them and taps, as the bridge's clips had it.
- Its patience is the website's `newPatience`, `stepPatience` and `outOfPatience`. The website has no tap, so the desk's bothers are a mapping chosen here and put to the author as an open question: a tap is a poke (a finger on the glass for half a second), a tap while an act still plays is being gone to, at 3% of its battery or less it is on its charger, and a shake is being picked up. Run out, it plays `tap_dizzy` or `glyph_glitch`, or nothing for do not disturb and for minding it, which have no act yet; then it is cross for 30 to 50 seconds as on the website, and answers taps with the website's own words for it ("It is still cross with you."). Measured against the committed art: tapped every second it glitches on the 6th tap, every 2.4 seconds it sulks on the 18th, and every 4 seconds never.
- The bridge (class `Mind`) starts the mind with the art the board or the emulator plays and sends its signals from where it used to decide (the commands, a Stop, a compaction, a hello and a failure from the hooks, a tap, the usage, waiting, idling), then plays the answer with `e <act>`. The act goes after the mood: sent before it, as the bridge sent its events, the act is lost on the board's way to the new mood (`settle` drops an event queued for a mood it passes through); on the emulator's board with a fake clock, `e done_glider` then `s done` played nothing, and `s done` then `e done_glider` played it. A name that is not `[a-z0-9_]` is not sent. If there is no Node, or the mind is not ready, does not answer in 300 ms or stops, the bridge does what it did before (Lump's clips and events) and logs the trouble once; a mind that stops, or does not answer three times running, is started again after 2 seconds, doubling up to 5 minutes. The hook is answered before any of this, so it never waits. `tools/install_hooks.py` installs a PreCompact hook as it does the others.
- Checked: `node --test` in `mind/`, 22 tests (the table, its routing as data, priorities, cooldowns, no repeats, weights over thousands of draws, the committed art's missing acts, each outcome of its patience, coming back left alone, the battery crossing a quarter, late at night, the protocol and its version, bad lines, and the contract against `mind/README.md`); the bridge's Python tests, 118 (the 93 there were, and 25 for the mind's path, its fallback, and the real process stopping, stalling, speaking another protocol or missing Node, and the contract from the bridge's side); the emulator's 44, unchanged. End to end (`node mind/checks/e2e.mjs`), twice: the real bridge in emulator mode with `firmware/data/sage.bin`, the real mind, the page in headless Chromium, hooks posted as the installed ones post them. A session after a long quiet played `hello`; a failing `npm test` played `glyph_glitch` both times (the bridge: "mind: tests -> glyph_glitch (tests failed; not in the art: life_tests_fail, holo_tests, holo_screen_tests)"); a PreCompact `glyph_decode`; a push `done_glider` ("not in the art: life_deploy"); a quiet Stop `glyph_joy`; the battery falling to 20% nothing ("the art holds none of holo_battery"); idle time `fidget_mark` in one run and `fidget_antenna` in the other; clicks on the prism a second apart five `tap_hello` and on the sixth `glyph_glitch` ("out of patience: a glitch"), and one more nothing ("It is still cross with you."). The first choice for a failing test is 75% `work_tests_fail` (measured over 4,000 fresh minds), so `glyph_glitch` both times was a one in sixteen draw; over a long run the two alternate more than their weights say (62% and 38%), the cost of no repeats with two acts.
- Not checked: the board (none yet); a real Claude Code session (the hooks were posted with curl as the installed ones post them; that a failing Bash command arrives as PostToolUseFailure is what the bridge has assumed since before the mind); the installed login item, whose copy of the bridge has no `mind/` beside it and so runs without it, and whose installer stops at Lump's sound scripts, which are gone; dizzy, do not disturb, minding it and late at night end to end (unit tests only: the board feels no shake, and the run was in the afternoon); and how the weights feel over days on the desk. The page drew 11.5 and 16.5 frames a second in those runs, under `nice -n 19` and polled by the check every 30 ms, against 23.9 in the emulator's own check: a number of this machine, not of the board.
